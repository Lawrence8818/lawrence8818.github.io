Here is a surprise many first-time new-launch buyers get. They sign the SPA, the loan is approved, and they assume nothing is payable until the keys arrive. Then, some months later, the bank starts deducting a small amount every month, and it keeps growing.

That is progressive interest. It is normal, it is not a hidden charge, and once you see how it works you can budget for it properly.

## Why it exists

When you buy a completed property, the bank pays the seller in one go and your full instalment starts.

When you buy under construction, the law does not allow the developer to collect the full price upfront. The developer bills in stages as the building goes up, following the payment schedule in your SPA. The bank releases your loan in the same stages.

You are only charged interest on what the bank has actually released. Little is released at the start, so the interest is small. As more is released, it grows.

## The stages (Schedule H)

For strata property such as condos and serviced residences sold under the standard Schedule H agreement, the Third Schedule of the SPA sets out the stages:

| Stage | % of price |
| --- | --- |
| On signing the SPA | 10% |
| Work below ground level | 10% |
| Structural framework of your unit | 15% |
| Walls, door and window frames | 10% |
| Roofing, wiring, plumbing | 10% |
| Internal and external plastering | 10% |
| Sewerage works | 5% |
| Drains | 2.5% |
| Roads | 2.5% |
| Vacant possession | 17.5% |
| Delivery of strata title | 2.5% |
| Held by the lawyer as stakeholder | 5% |

Landed property with individual title is sold under Schedule G. The idea and the staging are similar, but check the Third Schedule in your own SPA for the exact percentages and order.

The developer has up to 36 months to deliver a Schedule H property and 24 months for Schedule G, so the stages are spread over that period. They do not arrive at even intervals.

## A worked example

Take a RM500,000 condo with a 90% loan of RM450,000, at an illustrative rate of 4% a year. Your 10% covers the signing stage, then the bank starts releasing.

Monthly interest = amount released × 4% ÷ 12.

| After this stage is released | Total released by bank | Monthly interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work below ground (10%) | RM50,000 | about RM167 |
| Structural framework (15%) | RM125,000 | about RM417 |
| Walls (10%) | RM175,000 | about RM583 |
| Roofing, wiring (10%) | RM225,000 | about RM750 |
| Plastering (10%) | RM275,000 | about RM917 |
| Sewerage, drains, roads (10%) | RM325,000 | about RM1,083 |
| Vacant possession and balance (25%) | RM450,000 | full instalment starts |

For comparison, the full instalment on RM450,000 over 35 years at 4% is roughly RM1,990 a month.

If those stages were spread evenly over three years, the interest paid during construction would come to roughly RM20,000. Your real figure depends on your loan rate, your loan amount and how fast the developer builds. A faster build means the amounts step up sooner.

Two things to notice:

- This money is interest only. It does not reduce the RM450,000 you owe.
- If your loan is less than 90%, you pay the difference to the developer first, before the bank releases anything.

## What DIBS was, and why you will not see it now

Before 2014, many projects were sold with the Developer Interest Bearing Scheme. The developer paid the buyer's progressive interest during construction, so the buyer paid almost nothing until completion.

It sounded generous, but the cost was built into the selling price, and it made it very easy to buy on speculation with little cash. The government ended it in Budget 2014. Developers may not launch projects with DIBS features, and banks are not allowed to finance them.

So today, progressive interest is the buyer's cost. Developers still offer rebates and other incentives, but those are a different thing. If anyone tells you there is "no interest during construction", ask how exactly, and read it in the documents before you rely on it.

## How to budget for it

1. **Ask the banker for an estimate.** Give them the price and loan amount, and ask for the interest at each stage at the current rate.
2. **Plan for the overlap.** If you are renting now, you will be paying rent and progressive interest together, and the highest amounts come in the last year before the keys.
3. **Leave room for rate changes.** Most home loans are floating rate. The 4% above is only for illustration.
4. **Automate it.** The amount changes each time a stage is released, so a fixed transfer will fall short. Use a standing instruction that pays whatever is billed, and keep a buffer in the account.
5. **Ask where the project is now.** A project that is already halfway built means larger releases soon after you sign, and a shorter wait.
6. **Keep your cash for handover.** Do not let construction-period interest eat the money you set aside for legal fees, renovation and moving in.
